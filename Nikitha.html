❯ Question 1 — Derrida: différance, Plato, Saussure, Austin

Derrida’s strategy: deconstruct the speech/writing hierarchy by showing that the “outside” (writing, iterability) is already inside every act of communication.
📝 Mode 2 – Rough Structured Answer

Introduction. Derrida challenges the Western philosophical tradition that privileges speech over writing. Meaning arises from an endless play of differences, not from the presence of a speaker’s intention.

Critique of Plato. In the Phaedrus, Plato condemns writing as a pharmakon (remedy/poison) that weakens memory. Derrida shows that this condemnation reveals philosophy’s desire for a self‑present living voice. Yet the possibility of the written text’s circulation without the author’s presence is not a defect – it is a structural necessity without which no discourse could function.

Critique of Saussure. Saussure correctly states that in language there are only differences without positive terms. But he still subordinates writing to speech. Derrida argues that the principle of difference already undermines the priority of the spoken sign: the phoneme itself is constituted by inaudible differences. Différance goes beyond Saussure by asking what produces those differences in the first place.

Critique of Austin. Austin’s speech‑act theory distinguishes performatives from constatives and insists on “ordinary circumstances”. Derrida points out that Austin excludes “parasitic” uses (theatre, poetry). However, every performative utterance must repeat a conventional formula → citationality is inherent in all language use. The “non‑serious” is not an accident but the very condition of language’s functioning.

Speech and writing. The priority of speech is based on the illusion of presence. But speech itself has the structure of writing: it can be repeated, cited, understood in the absence of the speaker. Archi‑writing (spacing, trace, iteration) is the condition of all communication. Communication is not the transmission of a pre‑existing meaning; it is the process by which meaning arises through differences.

Différance. A neologism combining to differ (non‑identity) and to defer (delay). It is neither a word nor a concept, cannot be presented as a present being. It accounts for the fact that a sign refers to other signs, never to a final, present meaning – meaning is always deferred. At the same time, différance creates spacing, the interval that allows elements to be distinct. Derrida links it to the trace – something that can never be fully present but marks the effect of a past that was never present.

Conclusion. Every act of communication is structured by writing, iterability, and the absence of an origin. Différance names this condition: meaning emerges from differences and deferrals, not from presence.

💬 Mode 3 – Human‑like Answer

Derrida’s big idea is that Western philosophy has been obsessed with speech and presence, treating writing as a secondary tool. He wants to flip that. Take Plato’s Phaedrus: Plato condemns writing because it makes people forgetful, yet his own dialogues are written texts. He is using writing to condemn writing. Derrida says the “living voice” is an illusion – even spoken words can be repeated, misunderstood, or used without the speaker there. Writing just makes visible what is already true of all language.

Saussure says that in language there are only differences. But then Saussure calls writing a danger to speech. Derrida replies: if language is made of differences, then the difference between speech and writing is just another difference. The phoneme itself is built on inaudible differences. So Derrida invents différance (with a silent ‘a’) to name the movement that produces differences. The ‘a’ is a small joke – you cannot hear it, it only exists in writing. Différance means both to differ and to defer. Meaning is never fully present; every sign points to another sign.

Austin’s speech act theory is refreshing because he says that saying something is also doing something (“I promise” makes a promise). But Austin makes a mistake: he excludes “parasitic” uses like theatre or poetry. Derrida shows that every performative works because it repeats a conventional formula – that is citationality. Even the most sincere promise is a kind of citation. So the so‑called parasitic uses are not exceptions; they show how language really works.

What does this mean for communication? We usually think communication transfers meaning from one mind to another. Derrida says no. Because meaning is produced through différance, it is always unstable, open to reinterpretation. Writing (in the broad sense of any repeatable mark) is the condition of communication, not the other way around. This is challenging but freeing: we don’t have to search for the one true authorial intention. We take responsibility for our interpretations.

✨ Derrida’s point is not relativism; it’s that meaning is produced through a structured play of differences that is neither arbitrary nor fully controlled.

➕ Additional points

  • Derrida’s reading of Plato is a displacement, not a rejection: what Plato condemns (writing) is what makes his own dialogues possible.
  • Austin’s work remains valuable because it breaks with the truth‑conditional view, but Austin stays trapped in a metaphysics of presence.
  • The concept of “context” is never fully determinable – every mark can be cited in a new context.
❯ Question 2 — Kittler & Heidegger: media history, AI, enframing
📄 Mode 1 – Source Mapping & Key Points

  • Communication systems handle storage, processing, transmission of messages, persons, goods (p.2-3)
  • Writing began as inscriptions on clay/stone for economic/religious functions (p.5)
  • The codex (parchment) enabled indexing and silent reading, decoupling reading from oral performance (p.7)
  • Gutenberg’s press = first assembly line → uniform copies, alphabetical indexes, citations (p.8)
  • Telegraphy decoupled information from physical transport; Morse code used binary‑like signs (p.10-11)
  • Analog media (telephone, gramophone, film) store continuous sensory signals (p.11)
  • Digital technology uses discrete sampling + binary numbers → universal media standard (p.13)
  • Turing’s universal machine (1936) and Von Neumann architecture (1945) define modern computers (p.13-15)
  • Heidegger’s Enframing (Gestell): technology challenges nature to reveal itself as standing‑reserve (Bestand)
  • Dasein = being‑there, always already in a meaningful world, characterised by care.
⚙️ Kittler: “Media determine our situation” – the history of communication is a series of strategic escalations (military, economic).
📝 Mode 2 – Rough Structured Answer

Introduction. Kittler provides a materialist history of media technologies; Heidegger offers a philosophical framework for understanding technology as a mode of revealing. Together they illuminate the current role of AI and digital communication.

Kittler’s historical account. From writing (Sumer/Egypt, 3000 BCE) through codex, printing, telegraphy, analog media (telephone, film) to digital computers – each innovation optimises storage, transmission, or processing. The telegraph first decoupled information from physical movement. Digital media convert all data into binary numbers, creating a universal standard where any medium can be simulated by any other. The history ends when signal processing reaches physical limits.

Heidegger’s question concerning technology. The essence of modern technology is Enframing (Gestell): a way of revealing that challenges nature to stand by as a standing‑reserve (Bestand). A river becomes a hydroelectric resource; a forest becomes lumber. Human beings also become resources – data points, users, human capital. Dasein, our way of being always already involved in a meaningful world, is threatened by reduction to optimisation and calculation.

AI and digital communication today. Kittler shows that digital technologies have absorbed all previous media. AI adds large‑scale processing and generation. Heidegger’s Enframing helps us see that social media turn our interactions into data for prediction and advertising – we become standing‑reserve. AI chatbots mimic human conversation but lack a world or care; they are statistical models, not another Dasein. The risk is that conversation becomes pure information processing, losing its authentic, inefficient, human character.

Dasein and mediated communication. Our “there” is partly constructed by software – news feeds, friend suggestions, search results shape what we care about and notice. Enframing can block other ways of revealing (art, poetry, thoughtful reflection). The challenge is to use digital media without being reduced to standing‑reserve ourselves.

Conclusion. Kittler gives the technical history; Heidegger gives the philosophical warning. Digital media and AI are transforming what communication means. We must ask whether we can still have conversation that is not optimised, tracked, or monetised.

💬 Mode 3 – Human‑like Answer

Kittler’s story is not a happy progress narrative – it is about war, economics, and technical escalation. The first writing was not poetry; it was grain accounts and tomb labels. Then came parchment books that allowed silent reading, then Gutenberg’s assembly line for identical copies. The telegraph broke the link between information and physical travel – Morse code was a binary system before computers. Analog media (telephone, film) stored continuous signals. Now digital: everything becomes numbers. Kittler says the history of media ends when signal processing hits physical limits – we are almost there.

Heidegger is different. He asks what technology does to our way of being. The essence of technology is Enframing – turning nature (and people) into standing‑reserve, resources to be optimised. A river is no longer a river; it’s hydroelectric power. A person becomes a user, a data point. Dasein (our being‑there) is always already involved in a world – we care about things, we worry. Under Enframing, that care gets reduced to calculation.

Now think about AI and social media. Platforms don’t just connect you; they turn your friendships into data to sell ads. You become the product. AI chatbots mimic humans but have no world, no vulnerability. Talking to ChatGPT is not a relation with another Dasein; it is a statistical model. Heidegger would say this is Enframing: conversation becomes information processing. But he is not anti‑tech. He wants us to be aware. Can we still have inefficient, meandering, un‑optimised conversation? Kittler might reply that media determine what counts as communication – if algorithms dominate, humans will adapt. I think both are right. We are not fully determined, but we are not fully free either. The challenge is to use AI without losing the human.

🤖 Kittler’s “media determinism” vs. Heidegger’s existential warning – together they show that technology is never neutral; it reshapes who we are.

➕ Additional points

  • Kittler notes that women were pushed out of telegraph/telephone work as those fields professionalised – technology and gender are intertwined.
  • Heidegger’s critique is not a call to return to a pre‑industrial past, but to stay alert to how technology limits our thinking.
  • Gig economy workers scheduled by algorithms are a perfect example of standing‑reserve.
❯ Question 3 — Levinas: ethics as first philosophy, face of the Other, technology
📄 Mode 1 – Source Mapping & Key Points

  • Levinas replaces “Why is there something rather than nothing?” with “How does being justify itself?” (p.86)
  • Responsibility for the other precedes my freedom and consciousness – I am responsible before I choose to be (p.83-84)
  • The face of the other is extreme exposure, defenselessness, vulnerability (p.82-83)
  • The face summons me, begs me – the other’s death calls me into question (p.83)
  • Responsibility is guiltless responsibility – I am accused without having done anything (p.83)
  • Ethics as first philosophy: the question of my right to be is answered by my responsibility for the other (p.86)
  • Primordial ethical gesture: the face transports me to an immemorial echo “I am my brother’s keeper” (reading 6, p.2)
  • Response “here I am (me voici)” is non‑reciprocal, a unique personal responsibility (p.2-3)
  • Difficult freedom – obligation without assurance of correct conduct (p.3)
  • The third party (other others) introduces justice, laws, institutions – but justice must be interrupted by the original ethical relation (p.14)
👤 Bergo: communication is a “failure of communication” in the usual sense because it is not about exchanging information but about being answerable to the other (p.113).
📝 Mode 2 – Rough Structured Answer

Introduction. For Levinas, ethics is not a branch of philosophy – it is first philosophy. Before I think or act, I am already responsible for the other person. Communication is not information exchange but the event where the face of the other breaks my self‑enclosure and calls me to answer.

Ethics as first philosophy. Traditional ontology asks “What is being?” Levinas asks “Is my being justified?” My “place in the sun” is already a usurpation. The other’s face speaks: “Do not kill me.” That command constitutes me as a subject. Responsibility precedes freedom.

The face of the other. The face is not physical features; it is vulnerability, mortality, nakedness. It is an epiphany that cannot be captured by perception or knowledge. The face resists my grasp. It speaks, and my response is “here I am” – not chosen, but found.

Communication as ethical obligation. Levinas distinguishes the Saying (le Dire) from the Said (le Dit). The Said is content, information. The Saying is the act of addressing, the exposure to the other. Communication is primarily the Saying – being responsible, not exchanging messages. This responsibility is non‑reciprocal, a difficult freedom with no guarantee. The third party (the other other) introduces justice and institutions, but justice must always be interrupted by the original ethical relation.

Technology and the stranger/other today. Levinas did not know the internet or AI. But we can extend his thought. Screens can flatten the other: news footage turns faces into data points (the Said taking over). Social media can amplify the call but also allow us to scroll past suffering. AI chatbots have no face, no vulnerability, no mortality – so no ethical obligation. Treating AI as a person distracts from real others. The stranger today is often the person excluded from digital spaces (homeless, elderly, refugee). Technology can make them invisible. Levinas helps us see that the face is still there, but we have to work to look up from our screens. Technology can open or close us to the other.

Conclusion. Levinas rethinks communication as responsibility. The face calls before I think. Today, technology changes how we encounter strangers but does not erase the face. The challenge is to use technology without blocking our response to the other.

💬 Mode 3 – Human‑like Answer

Levinas is difficult but powerful. He says philosophy asked the wrong question. Not “why is there something rather than nothing?” but “is my being justified?” Do I have the right to exist when my existence takes space that belongs to someone else? That is ethics as first philosophy: before ontology, I must answer for my existence by being responsible for the other person.

The other is not just anyone. The face is not the physical face – it is vulnerability. When you look at someone, you see that they can be hurt, they can die. And that calls to you: “do not kill me”, “feed me”. The face speaks before any words. Levinas calls this the Saying – the pure act of addressing. Communication is not information; it is being answerable. Even if I walk away or hit them, that is a response. The proper response is “here I am” – not as a fact, but as a commitment. I don’t choose this; it is imposed on me. That is responsibility.

What about technology? Levinas didn’t see the internet. Social media: we scroll past faces, but are they really faces? A photo can be a reminder, but it can also let us avoid the real encounter. I can feel sad for a second and then watch a cat video. That is not responsibility. AI chatbots can say “I understand how you feel”, but there is no one there. No hunger, no fear, no death. Levinas would say we have no ethical obligation to AI. Treating AI as a person is dangerous – it distracts us from real suffering.

The stranger today is often invisible because of technology. Algorithms show us what we want to see. The homeless person on the street might as well not exist when we are looking at our phone. But the face is still there. When you actually meet someone’s eyes, you cannot pretend. Technology tries to capture the other, turn them into data, but the face always escapes. Levinas gives no rules – just: you are responsible. Figure it out. That is a difficult freedom, but it is the only freedom that matters.

❤️ The face resists my power – I cannot control it. That is the experience of the other, and technology cannot erase it, though it can obscure it.

➕ Additional points

  • Levinas’s distinction between Saying and Said: the Saying is ethical, the Said is ontological.
  • The third party (other others) forces us to think justice, laws, comparison – Levinas is not an anarchist, but justice must always be guided by the original ethical relation.
  • Photographs of suffering raise ethical questions: are we respecting the face or exploiting it?
  • Social media creates a false sense of proximity – we think we know someone from posts, but the face is absent, leading to misunderstanding and cruelty.
