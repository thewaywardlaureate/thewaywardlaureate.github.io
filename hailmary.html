# Chapter 1 — History of Neuroscience and Neurophysiology

1.1 The Human Quest to Find the Source of Bio-electricity

Long before the cell membrane or the ion channel was known, natural philosophers wondered whether the “vital force” that animated living tissue was itself electrical. Two 18th-century demonstrations frame this quest [Deck I, Slide 5]:

  • Electrifying-boy experiment (18th century): A boy suspended on insulating silk cords was charged with static electricity from a friction machine; when a girl standing on an insulating stool touched him, a spark jumped between them. This was designed to demonstrate the presence of a hypothetical “conducting fluid” called ether, and to show that a charge could be transmitted through a living body.
  • Galvanism / “A Galvanised Corpse” (gothic 18th-century demonstrations): Electrical stimulation of executed criminals’ corpses to see whether electricity could restore movement or “life.” These sensational public demonstrations fed directly into the cultural imagination (Mary Shelley’s Frankenstein, or the Modern Prometheus) and into serious science.

1.1.1 Subsection: Why this matters for the syllabus

These demonstrations set the stage for the central question of neurophysiology: is the signal that runs through nerve and muscle electrical, and if so, what generates it? The answer (ionic gradients + selective permeability, covered in Chapters 4–6) took roughly 150 years to work out rigorously after Galvani’s first experiments.

1.2 Luigi Galvani and the Birth of “Animal Electricity”

[Deck I, Slide 6–7]

  • Luigi Galvani is called the “Father of Neurophysiology.”

  • In 1791, Galvani published “Commentary on the electrical force of muscular motion” (De Viribus Electricitatis in Motu Musculari Commentarius), describing experiments in which a frog’s sciatic nerve–leg muscle preparation twitched when touched by two different metals connected together (or exposed to atmospheric electricity), even with no external battery.

  • Galvani interpreted this as evidence that electricity was inherent to the animal itself — a “nerveo-electrical fluid” generated within the animal and conducted like the discharge of a Leyden jar. His own words (quoted in the deck):

    “These results surprised us greatly and led us to suspect that the electricity was inherent in the animal itself… similar to the electric circuit which is completed in a Leyden jar…” — Luigi Galvani, 1791 (cited via A. Mauro, Journal of the History of Medicine and Allied Sciences, Oxford University Press, 1969)

  • Giovanni Aldini (Galvani’s nephew) extended these demonstrations dramatically to human corpses, exploiting the 1751 Murder Act in Britain, which permitted scientists to experiment on the corpses of executed prisoners. These “galvanism” demonstrations showed limbs twitching and faces grimacing under electrical stimulation, and are historically linked to the inspiration for Frankenstein.

1.2.1 Galvani vs. Volta: the animal-electricity controversy [Supplemented]

  • Alessandro Volta disputed Galvani’s “animal electricity” interpretation, arguing instead that the electricity arose from the contact of two dissimilar metals (bimetallic effect) and that the frog leg was merely a sensitive detector, not a source. This dispute (the Galvani–Volta controversy) directly motivated Volta’s invention of the voltaic pile (the first true battery) in 1800.
  • Modern understanding vindicates both, in a sense: Volta was right that metal-metal contact potentials can generate current artifactually, but Galvani was also right that biological tissue does generate its own bioelectricity — via ionic gradients across membranes (the subject of the rest of this course).

1.4 The Membrane World — Origin-of-Life Context [Deck I, Slide 16–17]

The slides frame the “mother of all questions” — where did we come from, how did life originate on Earth? — around several competing/complementary hypotheses, because the plasma membrane is treated in this course as “the perceptron”, i.e., the fundamental information-processing unit that had to evolve before excitable cells (and hence neurons) could exist:

  1. Life in the prebiotic soup: Miller–Urey experiment (Nobel context: origin-of-life chemistry, 1934 reference in slide relates to earlier amino-acid synthesis ideas) — sparking amino acids/organics from a simulated early-Earth atmosphere.
  2. RNA World: Thomas Cech’s discovery of ribozymes (Cell, 1982; Nobel 1989) and Walter Gilbert’s “RNA World” hypothesis (Nature, 1986; Nobel 1980) — RNA as both information carrier and catalyst, potentially predating proteins and DNA.
  3. Metabolism First: Peter Mitchell (Nobel 1978) — chemiosmotic H⁺ gradient-driven ATP synthesis; life may have originated using proton gradients at deep-sea hydrothermal vents, i.e., metabolism (energy transduction) preceded genetics.
  4. Membrane First: Pier Luigi Luisi’s protocell work (The Emergence of Life – From Chemical Origins to Synthetic Biology, 2016) — self-assembling lipid vesicles as the first compartments.
  5. Division of protocells: Jack Szostak (Nobel 2009) — demonstrating that simple fatty-acid vesicles can grow and divide, a plausible proto-cellular reproduction mechanism (Blain & Szostak, Annu. Rev. Biochem., 2014; Szostak, Nature, 2018).
  6. Protocells with pyrite & RNA: John Sutherland & Jack Szostak — protocells encapsulating RNA with iron-sulfur mineral (pyrite) chemistry as an energy source (O’Flaherty et al., JACS, 2019).

1.4.1 Subsection: Why origin-of-life material appears in a neurophysiology course

The course’s logic (see Section 1.5, “Hierarchical Organization”) is that the plasma membrane is the ancestral computational device: once a lipid bilayer could separate charge and support selective ion permeability, it created the physical substrate for membrane potentials, which is the raw material that excitable cells (neurons, muscle) later specialized into rapid electrical signaling.

1.6 Neurons Exhibit a Myriad of Electrical Signals [Deck I, Slide 8]

Even though all neurons obey the same basic biophysics (Chapters 4–6), different neuron types display strikingly different firing patterns, illustrating the physiological diversity built on a common electrochemical substrate:

  • Stuttering (d-STUT) — bursts of spikes separated by irregular gaps
  • Accommodating (c-AC) — spike frequency decreases (adapts) during a sustained stimulus
  • Bursting (r-BST) — rhythmic clusters (“bursts”) of action potentials
  • Non-accommodating (b-NAC) — sustained, roughly constant-frequency firing throughout the stimulus

These patterns are recorded from a heterogeneous cortical column (mixture of excitatory pyramidal neurons in red and inhibitory interneurons in blue) (Sahar Farajnia et al., J. Neurosci., 2012).

1.6.1 Pathological electrical signatures [Deck I, Slide 8]

  • Paroxysmal Depolarization Shift (PDS): a large, sustained depolarization with a burst of action potentials riding on top — the cellular electrophysiological hallmark of epileptiform activity.
  • Altered firing patterns in aging / Alzheimer’s disease: the slide contrasts “Young-Day,” “Old-Day,” “Young-Night,” “Old-Night” firing traces, showing that circadian and age-related changes alter neuronal excitability patterns — relevant to Alzheimer’s disease pathophysiology.

1.7 The Crowded Cell [Deck I, Slide 9]

A conceptual/visual point (from the “Inner Life of the Cell,” Biovision, Harvard University animation) — the cytoplasm and membrane are not an empty aqueous compartment with a few sparse proteins; they are extremely crowded with proteins, vesicles, cytoskeletal filaments, and organelles. This crowding is physiologically important because: - It affects diffusion coefficients (Chapter 4) — real intracellular diffusion is slower than in free/bulk solution. - It underlies the physical need for active transport machinery (motor proteins, vesicular trafficking) rather than relying purely on passive diffusion over long distances (e.g., along an axon).

1.8 What Is So Special About Neurons? Excitable vs. Non-Excitable Cells [Deck I, Slide 11]

Starting from an undifferentiated stem cell, differentiation can produce: - Cardiomyocyte — excitable, generates a characteristic action-potential-linked “field potential” signal - Smooth muscle cell — excitable, generates its own characteristic field potential - Neuron — excitable, generates its own characteristic field potential (fast, sharp spikes) - Other somatic cell (e.g., fibroblast) — non-excitable, shows no signal

Key concept: “Excitability” is not a property of cells in general — it is a specialized property conferred by expression of specific voltage-gated ion channels (Nav, Cav, Kv) that allow rapid, regenerative, all-or-none electrical responses (the action potential). Each excitable cell type has a unique field-potential signature because of differences in the specific complement of channels it expresses.

1.9 Overview of Neural Information Processing in the CNS [Deck I, Slide 12]

  • The Upper Motor Neuron (UMN) pathway: primary motor cortex → pyramidal cells → internal capsule → corticospinal tract → cerebral peduncle/crus of midbrain → pyramids of medulla oblongata → decussation in pyramids (most fibers cross to the opposite side) → lateral corticospinal tract → decussation in spinal cord (remaining fibers) → Lower Motor Neuron (LMN) → peripheral nerve → neuromuscular junction → skeletal muscle.
  • The example given (a batsman playing a cricket shot) illustrates that voluntary, planned movement requires cortical areas (premotor cortex, motor cortex, posterior parietal cortex, visual cortices) and subcortical structures (basal ganglia, cerebellum, brainstem nuclei, hippocampus, amygdala, hypothalamus) working together.
  • Core physiological principle stated on the slide: “Communication and information processing in the Nervous system is by electrical signals (I or V)” — i.e., current (I) and voltage (V), which is precisely the subject matter developed quantitatively in Chapters 4–6.

1.10 The Cell as an Electrical Circuit — Conceptual Bridge (Cell ↔︎ Computer analogy) [Deck I, Slide 13, “hand-drawn” figure]

The lecture draws a deliberate analogy between a eukaryotic cell and a computer: - Nucleus (DNA) ↔︎ hard disk (long-term information storage) - Endoplasmic reticulum (ER) ↔︎ bus (the “ER-bus” — information/molecule transport network) - Mitochondria ↔︎ power supply (ATP generation = “POWER”) - A generic cell membrane patch with R (resistance) and C (capacitance) in parallel, driven by a voltage V ↔︎ the CPU circuit board, foreshadowing the equivalent-circuit model of the membrane (Section 3.6 / Chapter 6).

This is a pedagogical device: it previews that the electrical equivalent circuit (R, C, V — resistor, capacitor, battery) is the right level of abstraction for modeling how neurons compute and communicate.

# Chapter 2 — Cell and Molecular Biology of Neurons

Note: The uploaded decks are focused mainly on membrane biophysics rather than classical neuronal cell biology, so this chapter is substantially [Supplemented] with standard textbook material, cross-referenced to deck concepts (the “crowded cell,” protein/lipid composition, excitable vs. non-excitable cells) wherever they connect.

2.1 The Neuron as a Specialized, Polarized Cell [Supplemented; connects to Deck I Slide 11]

A typical neuron has four functionally and structurally distinct compartments:

  1. Dendrites — branched processes that receive synaptic input; contain ligand-gated ion channels (postsynaptic receptors) at synapses and can have varying densities of voltage-gated channels for local dendritic integration/amplification.
  2. Cell body (soma) — contains the nucleus and the bulk of the biosynthetic machinery (rough ER, Golgi, ribosomes); integrates synaptic inputs; site of the axon hillock, where action potentials are typically initiated because of its high density of voltage-gated Na⁺ channels and low spike threshold.
  3. Axon — a long, thin process specialized for long-distance, high-fidelity signal propagation via the action potential; may be myelinated or unmyelinated.
  4. Axon terminal / presynaptic bouton — contains synaptic vesicles filled with neurotransmitter, voltage-gated Ca²⁺ channels that trigger vesicle fusion, and the release machinery (SNARE proteins).

2.2 The Crowded Cytoplasm and Membrane [Deck I, Slide 9]

As emphasized in Deck I, the “Inner Life of the Cell” visualization shows the cell interior is densely packed with: - Cytoskeletal filaments (microtubules, actin, intermediate filaments/neurofilaments) - Motor proteins (kinesin, dynein — walking along microtubules) - Vesicles in transit - Ribosomes, ER, Golgi, mitochondria

This crowding has direct biophysical consequences for diffusion (Chapter 4) — intracellular diffusion coefficients are lower than free-solution values, and long-range transport of proteins/organelles in neurons (which can have axons up to a meter long in humans) generally requires active, motor-driven axonal transport rather than passive diffusion.

2.2.1 Axonal transport [Supplemented]

  • Anterograde transport (soma → terminal): driven by kinesin motors along microtubules; carries synaptic vesicle precursors, mitochondria, membrane proteins.
  • Retrograde transport (terminal → soma): driven by dynein motors; carries signaling endosomes, degraded proteins, and (pathologically) some neurotropic viruses and toxins (e.g., tetanus toxin, rabies virus) back to the cell body.
  • Clinically relevant example: axonal transport failure is implicated in neurodegenerative diseases (e.g., accumulation of tau, amyloid — connects to the “Alzheimer’s” firing-pattern slide in Chapter 1).

2.3 The Plasma Membrane as “The Perceptron” [Deck I, Slide 17]

The lecture explicitly frames the plasma membrane as analogous to a perceptron (the simplest unit of a neural network in machine learning) — i.e., a device that: - Receives multiple inputs (ions, ligands, mechanical force, voltage) across its surface, - Integrates them via embedded intrinsic membrane proteins (ion channels, receptors, transporters), and - Produces an output (a change in membrane potential, or a downstream signaling cascade).

The membrane itself is built from: - Phospholipid bilayer — amphipathic phospholipids self-assemble so hydrophilic head groups face the aqueous environment (both extracellular fluid and cytosol) and hydrophobic acyl-chain tails face each other, forming a bilayer core impermeant to ions and other charged/polar solutes. - Intrinsic (integral) membrane proteins — span the bilayer (ion channels, transporters, receptors); allow the membrane to be selectively permeable. - The evolutionary starting point is proposed to be a protocell: a simple lipid micelle/vesicle (as in Szostak’s protocell work, Chapter 1) that could enclose and concentrate a set of molecules — the prerequisite for a cell at all.

2.4 Building Blocks: Lipids, Proteins, and the Membrane Skeleton [Supplemented]

2.4.1 Membrane lipids

  • Phosphoglycerides (e.g., phosphatidylcholine, phosphatidylserine, phosphatidylethanolamine) — the majority membrane lipid class.
  • Sphingolipids (e.g., sphingomyelin) — enriched in lipid rafts, cholesterol-rich membrane microdomains that can cluster specific ion channels/receptors.
  • Cholesterol — modulates membrane fluidity; also directly modulates the gating of several ion channels (e.g., some K⁺ channels, nAChR).
  • Lipid asymmetry: phosphatidylserine and phosphatidylethanolamine are normally confined to the inner (cytoplasmic) leaflet by ATP-dependent flippases; loss of this asymmetry (PS exposure on the outer leaflet) is a hallmark of apoptosis.

2.4.2 Membrane and cytoskeletal proteins

  • Integral membrane proteins: ion channels, transporters/carriers, pumps (ATPases), receptors (ligand-gated channels, GPCRs, receptor tyrosine kinases).
  • Peripheral membrane proteins: proteins associated with the membrane surface via protein-protein interactions or lipid anchors (e.g., ankyrin, spectrin — the sub-membrane cytoskeleton that anchors and clusters ion channels at specialized sites like the axon initial segment and nodes of Ranvier).
  • Cytoskeleton (Section 3.5 for detail): microtubules, actin filaments, and neurofilaments (the neuron-specific intermediate filament) provide structural support, define cell polarity (axon vs. dendrite), and serve as tracks for transport.

2.5 Membrane Protein Domain Architecture of Ion Channels [Deck II, Slide 22]

A key molecular-biology concept for understanding channel diversity: different ion channel families have characteristic transmembrane (TM) domain architectures, encoded by their genes. Examples from the deck (Shen & Pfaffinger, Handbook of Membrane Channels, 1994):

Take-home concept: channel function (which ion, what triggers gating) correlates strongly with TM domain count and cytosolic domain architecture — a recurring exam theme.

2.6 Ion Channels Distributed Across Multiple Organelles, Not Just the Plasma Membrane [Deck I, Slide 19]

Ion channels are classified not only by gating mechanism but also by subcellular localization: - Cell membrane — VGICs, LGICs, MGICs, leak channels (the “classical” excitability channels) - Endoplasmic Reticulum (ER) — IP₃R, RyR (intracellular Ca²⁺ release channels) - Mitochondria — mitochondrial ion channels regulate Ca²⁺ uptake, apoptosis signaling, and the permeability transition pore - Nucleus — nuclear envelope also contains ion channels (regulating nucleoplasmic Ca²⁺ and gene expression) - Endosome — channels on endosomal membranes (e.g., TRP channels) regulate endosomal ion content/pH

This is a molecular-cell-biology point: excitability and Ca²⁺ signaling are not confined to the plasma membrane — they are a whole-cell phenomenon coordinated across organelles.

2.7 Excitable vs. Non-Excitable Cell Fates from a Common Stem Cell [Deck I, Slide 11]

Revisited here from the molecular-biology angle: differentiation programs (transcription factor cascades) determine which set of ion channel genes a given cell type expresses. The same undifferentiated stem cell can give rise to: - Cardiomyocytes (excitable; distinct action potential shape due to L-type Ca²⁺ channels contributing a “plateau” phase) - Smooth muscle (excitable, but with different channel complement and typically graded, not all-or-none, responses) - Neurons (excitable; classic fast Na⁺/K⁺-driven spike) - Other somatic cells (non-excitable; lack the voltage-gated Na⁺/Ca²⁺ channel complement needed for regenerative spikes)

Core statement from the deck: “Ion channels conduct specific ions down their concentration gradients.” This single sentence is the physiological essence of excitability: channels are gated (opened) selectively, and once open, ions flow passively down their own electrochemical gradient (no direct energy expenditure by the channel itself — energy was spent earlier, by pumps, to set up the gradient).

3.2 Ion Channels of Excitable Cells — Functional Survey [Deck I, Slide 18]

Organized by ion selectivity, key channel families found in the plasma membrane of excitable cells:

3.2.1 Sodium channels

  • VGSC (voltage-gated sodium channel, Naᵥ) — generates the fast depolarizing upstroke of the action potential.
  • NALCN (Na⁺ leak channel, non-selective) — contributes to resting Na⁺ leak conductance.
  • ENaC (epithelial Na⁺ channel) — constitutively active/mechanically or hormonally regulated, important in epithelia (e.g., kidney) but present in some neurons too.

3.2.2 Calcium channels

  • VGCC (voltage-gated Ca²⁺ channel, Caᵥ) — Ca²⁺ influx triggers processes like neurotransmitter release, muscle contraction, gene transcription.
  • TRP (transient receptor potential channels) — polymodal, many gated by temperature, lipids, or mechanical stimuli.
  • P2X — ATP-gated (purinergic ligand-gated) cation channel, permeable to Ca²⁺ (and Na⁺).
  • Orai — store-operated Ca²⁺ channel, activated when ER Ca²⁺ stores are depleted (coupled to STIM1 in the ER membrane).

3.2.3 Potassium channels

  • VGKC (Kᵥ) — voltage-gated, contributes to repolarization.
  • K2P (two-pore-domain K⁺ leak channels, e.g., TREK1) — contribute to the resting K⁺ leak conductance and background excitability.
  • KCa — Ca²⁺-activated K⁺ channels (link intracellular Ca²⁺ signaling to membrane excitability).
  • IRK (inward-rectifier K⁺ channels) — conduct K⁺ more readily inward than outward; help stabilize resting potential.

3.2.4 Chloride channels

  • CFTR — cAMP/PKA-regulated Cl⁻ channel (an ABC-transporter-family channel; mutated in cystic fibrosis).
  • ClC — voltage-gated Cl⁻ channel/transporter family.
  • VRAC (volume-regulated anion channel) — activated by cell swelling.
  • LGCC (ligand-gated Cl⁻ channel, e.g., GABA_A, glycine receptors) — mediates fast inhibitory synaptic transmission.
  • CaCC (Ca²⁺-activated Cl⁻ channel).

(Azimi and Monteith, Endocrine-related Cancer, 2016 — figure source for this taxonomy)

# Chapter 4 — Physical Principles of Neuronal Excitability: Diffusion, Electro-diffusion, Nernst-Planck Equation, Donnan Equilibrium, Goldman-Hodgkin-Katz Equation

4.1 Diffusion — Fick’s Laws

4.1.1 Qualitative picture [Deck I, Slide 25]

Materials diffuse from higher concentration to lower concentration, driven by random thermal (Brownian) motion of particles, until the system reaches a homogeneous equilibrium (\(t=0\): inhomogeneous; \(t=\infty\): homogeneous solution).

4.1.2 Fick’s First Law [Deck I, Slides 25–27]

\[ J_{diff} = -D \frac{\partial [C]}{\partial x} \]

  • \(J_{diff}\) = diffusion flux (molecules/cm²·s) — amount of substance crossing unit area per unit time
  • \(D\) = diffusion coefficient / diffusivity (cm²/s)
  • \([C]\) = concentration (molecules/cm³ or mol/L)
  • The negative sign indicates flow is down the concentration gradient (from high to low).

Thermodynamic derivation (Deck I, Slides 26–27): Flow of solute is governed by its partial molar Gibbs free energy (chemical potential):

\[ \mu_i = \left(\frac{\partial G}{\partial n_i}\right)_{T,P,n_{j\ne i}} \]

The force per particle is \(-\frac{1}{N}\frac{\partial \mu_i}{\partial x}\), and velocity \(v_i = -\frac{NL_i}{C_i}F_i\), where \(L_i\) is a generalized conductivity.

Using Stokes’ law for frictional drag, \(f = 6\pi\eta R\) (η = viscosity, R = particle radius), and the Einstein relation, one derives:

\[ D = \frac{RT}{Nf} \]

Combining with the ideal-solution chemical potential \(\mu_2 = \mu_0 + RT\ln C_2\), Fick’s First Law falls out as a consequence of free-energy minimization, not just an empirical rule:

\[ J_2 = -\frac{RT}{Nf_2}\frac{\partial C_2}{\partial x} = -D_2\frac{\partial C_2}{\partial x} \]

where \(D_2 = RT/Nf_2\) is the diffusion coefficient.

4.1.3 Fick’s Second Law (Equation of Continuity) [Deck I, Slide 28]

Derived from mass conservation across a thin slab of cross-sectional area \(A\) and thickness \(\Delta x\):

\[ \frac{\partial C_i}{\partial t} = -\frac{\partial J_i}{\partial x} \]

Combining with Fick’s First Law:

\[ \frac{\partial C_2}{\partial t} = D_2\frac{\partial^2 C_2}{\partial x^2} \]

This is a diffusion (heat-type / parabolic PDE) equation — it tells you how concentration profiles evolve in space and time.

4.1.4 Solution of Fick’s Second Law & Random-Walk / Brownian Motion Connection [Deck I, Slides 29–30]

For a step-boundary initial condition (two solutions mixed in a long cylinder, sharp interface at \(x=0,\ t=0\)), the solution is a Gaussian error function profile, and its derivative is a Gaussian:

\[ \frac{\partial C_2}{\partial x} = \frac{C_0}{2(\pi D t)^{1/2}} e^{-x^2/4Dt} \]

The root-mean-square displacement of a diffusing particle:

\[ \overline{x^2} = 2Dt \quad \Longrightarrow \quad x_{rms} = \sqrt{2Dt} \]

Key physical insight (Einstein’s random-walk theory): diffusion is the macroscopic manifestation of the random Brownian motion of individual molecules — each molecule takes a random-direction step with equal probability in every direction; the RMS displacement scales as the square root of time (not linearly), which is why diffusion is an efficient transport mechanism only over short distances (e.g., across a synaptic cleft, ~20 nm) but hopelessly slow over long distances (e.g., along a 1-meter axon) — this is precisely why active axonal transport (Section 2.2.1) and regenerative electrical signaling (the action potential, Chapter 6) are necessary.

4.2 Electro-diffusion and the Nernst-Planck Equation (NPE)

4.2.1 Ohm’s Law of Drift Under an Electric Field [Deck I, Slide 31]

Charged particles (ions) in an electric field \(E\) experience a drift force, giving a drift flux:

\[ J_{drift} = \partial_{el} E = -\mu z [C]\frac{\partial V}{\partial x} \]

where \(\mu\) = mobility (cm²/V·s), \(z\) = valence, \([C]\) = concentration, \(V\) = electric potential, and \(E = -\partial V/\partial x\).

4.2.2 The Nernst-Planck Equation [Deck I, Slides 31–32; Deck II, Slide 8–11 re-derives the flux form]

Einstein’s random-walk theory shows that the same frictional resistance governs both drift and diffusion, so the two flux components are additive:

\[ J = J_{drift} + J_{diff} = -\mu z[C]\frac{\partial V}{\partial x} - D\frac{\partial [C]}{\partial x} \qquad \text{...(NPE, flux form)} \]

Using \(D = \frac{kT}{q}\mu\) (Einstein relation) and converting to molar units (\(u\) = molar mobility, \(N\) = Avogadro’s number, \(R\) = gas constant, \(F\) = Faraday’s constant):

\[ \mathbf{J} = -uz[C]\frac{\partial V}{\partial x} - u\frac{RT}{F}\frac{\partial [C]}{\partial x} \]

Multiplying by molar charge \(zF\) gives the current-density form of the NPE:

\[ \boxed{I = -\left(uz^2 F[C]\frac{\partial V}{\partial x} + uzRT\frac{\partial [C]}{\partial x}\right)} \]

  • \(u\) = molar mobility (cm²/V·s·mol)
  • The negative sign: current \(I\) flows opposite to \(\partial V/\partial x\), and (for a cation, \(z>0\)) opposite to \(\partial [C]/\partial x\).

4.2.3 Nernst Equation and Reversal (Equilibrium) Potential [Deck I, Slide 33]

At electrochemical equilibrium, the chemical gradient exactly balances the electrical gradient and net current is zero. Setting \(I=0\) in the NPE and integrating:

\[ V_2 - V_1 = -\frac{RT}{zF}\ln\frac{[C]_2}{[C]_1} \]

This is the Nernst Equation. The membrane potential at which net current for a given ion is zero is called the Nernst equilibrium potential (or reversal potential) for that ion.

Practical form (base-10 log, body temperature 37°C, monovalent ion):

\[ V_{ion} = 62\,\text{mV} \times \log_{10}\left(\frac{[ion]_{out}}{[ion]_{in}}\right) \]

(For an anion, the sign is flipped, or equivalently \(z=-1\) is used directly.)

4.2.4 Worked Numerical Example — Nernst Potentials for a Cat Motor Neuron [Deck I, Slide 34]

Given concentrations (mol/m³): Na⁺: in 15, out 150; K⁺: in 150, out 5.5; Cl⁻: in 9, out 125. (Resting \(V_m = -70\) mV at 37°C.)

\[ V_{Na^+} = 62\log_{10}\left(\frac{150}{15}\right) = 62\log_{10}(10) = +62\text{ mV} \]

\[ V_{K^+} = 62\log_{10}\left(\frac{5.5}{150}\right) = -89\text{ mV} \]

\[ V_{Cl^-} = -62\log_{10}\left(\frac{125}{9}\right) = -70\text{ mV} \]

Key point emphasized in the deck: Only a slight ionic imbalance is needed to generate a large potential difference. Numerically: if just 1/100,000th of the available cytosolic K⁺ ions crossed the membrane of a 10 µm-diameter spherical cell, the membrane potential would change by ~100 mV. This illustrates that membrane potential changes involve negligible bulk concentration changes — the ions that move are essentially only those that accumulate in a thin layer immediately adjacent to the membrane (capacitor charge), not a bulk-solution re-equilibration.

4.3 Donnan Equilibrium

4.3.1 Setup [Deck I, Slides 38–39]

Consider two compartments separated by a membrane permeable to K⁺ and Cl⁻ but impermeable to a large anion A⁻ (e.g., intracellular proteins/organic phosphates). At equilibrium, the membrane potential must simultaneously satisfy the Nernst equation for both permeant ions (K⁺ and Cl⁻) — this constraint, plus electroneutrality, defines the Donnan equilibrium.

General Donnan relation (for ions of valence \(+m\) and \(-n\)):

\[ \left[\frac{C^{+m}_{out}}{C^{+m}_{in}}\right]^{1/m} = \left[\frac{C^{-n}_{in}}{C^{-n}_{out}}\right]^{1/n} \]

Derivation for monovalent K⁺/Cl⁻ with impermeant intracellular anion A⁻:

Charge balance inside: \([K^+]_{in} = [Cl^-]_{in} + [A^-]_{in}\) Charge balance outside: \([K^+]_o = [Cl^-]_o\)

Setting the K⁺ and Cl⁻ Nernst potentials equal (both equal the membrane potential) leads to:

\[ [K^+]_o \times [Cl^-]_o = [Cl^-]_{in} \times [K^+]_{in} \]

\[ \Longrightarrow [K^+]_o^2 = [K^+]_{in}^2 - [K^+]_{in}\cdot[A^-]_{in} \]

Physiological consequence: the presence of impermeant intracellular anions (proteins) forces an asymmetric distribution of permeant ions (more K⁺ inside, more Cl⁻ outside) even at true equilibrium — and generates a resting Donnan potential across the membrane, and also creates an osmotic imbalance (more total solute particles on one side), which cells must counteract (this is one physiological rationale for needing active pumps like Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase — otherwise cells would swell and lyse via the “Donnan effect”).

4.3.2 Worked Example Setup [Deck I, Slide 40]

Two-compartment system, membrane permeable to K⁺ and Cl⁻, impermeant to A⁻:

Ion Compartment I (mM) Compartment II (mM)

Cl⁻ 50 150

Question posed: is this system at electrochemical equilibrium? (Check: is \([K^+]_I \times [Cl^-]_I = [K^+]_{II}\times[Cl^-]_{II}\)? → \(150\times50 = 7500\) vs. \(150\times150=22500\) — not equal, so the system is not at equilibrium; net ion flow will occur until the Donnan product equalizes on both sides, meaning K⁺ and Cl⁻ will flow from compartment II into compartment I until equilibrium is reached.)

4.4 Goldman-Hodgkin-Katz (GHK) Equation

4.4.1 Motivation — Shortcomings of the Nernst-Planck Equation [Deck I, Slide 7 of GHK section / Deck II, Slide 7]

  • NPE (as a simple drift-diffusion equation) is strictly only applicable to bulk solution, not to the complex environment inside a membrane.
  • NPE implicitly assumes Ohmic current-voltage behavior (\(V = IR\), linear \(I\)–\(V\)).
  • Real ion channels show deviation from Ohmic behavior — this is called rectification: outward rectification (channel conducts more easily outward than inward) or inward rectification (conducts more easily inward).
  • To account for this non-linearity, Goldman (1943) developed the constant-field theory. Historically notable: in 1943, the physical concept of a discrete “ion channel” protein was not yet established — Goldman derived this purely from continuum electrodiffusion theory.

4.4.2 The Constant-Field Assumption [Deck I/II, Slide 8]

The constant-field membrane model assumes the electric field inside the membrane is uniform (constant), i.e., the potential decays linearly across the membrane thickness \(l\):

\[ E = -\frac{\partial V}{\partial x} = -\frac{V}{l} \]

4.4.3 Permeability (P) — Derivation [Deck II, Slides 9–10]

Permeability is defined via: \(J = -P\Delta C\)

Starting from Fick’s first law with a partition coefficient \(\beta\) (water-membrane partition coefficient) relating the concentration just inside the membrane to the bulk aqueous concentration (\([C]_{x=0}=\beta C_{in}\), \([C]_{x=l}=\beta C_{out}\)), and diffusion coefficient inside the membrane \(D^*\):

\[ J = -D^*\frac{\beta \Delta C}{l} \quad \Longrightarrow \quad P = \frac{\beta D^*}{l} \]

Using the Einstein relation \(D^* = \frac{u_q^*RT}{F}\) (where \(u_q^*\) is the mobility factor for charged particles):

\[ \boxed{P = \frac{\beta u_q^* RT}{lF}} \]

4.4.4 Deriving the GHK Current Equation [Deck II, Slides 10–12]

Starting from the NPE current equation and substituting the constant-field assumption \(E=-V/l\):

\[ I = -\left(uz^2F[C]\frac{V}{l} + uzRT\frac{\partial[C]}{\partial x}\right) \]

Solving this first-order ODE in \([C]\) at steady state (\(I\) = constant, independent of \(x\)) and applying boundary conditions \([C]_{x=0}=\beta[C]_{in}\), \([C]_{x=l}=\beta[C]_{out}\), leads (after integration) to the GHK current equation:

\[ \boxed{I = PzF\varsigma\left[\frac{[C]_{in} - [C]_{out}e^{-\varsigma}}{1-e^{-\varsigma}}\right]} \]

where \(\varsigma = \dfrac{zVF}{RT}\) (a dimensionless normalized voltage) and \(P\) is the permeability defined above.

This equation describes a genuinely non-linear (rectifying) I–V relationship, in contrast to Ohmic \(V=IR\) behavior — this is the entire physical point of the GHK treatment (see the I–V curve figure, Deck II Slide 13, showing curves for different concentration ratios all deviating from the straight Ohmic line, producing outward or inward rectification depending on parameters).

4.4.5 The Goldman (GHK) Voltage Equation — Resting Membrane Potential for Multiple Permeant Ions [Deck I, Slide 14 of GHK section / Deck II, Slide 14]

For a membrane permeable to \(M\) negative ionic species and \(N\) positive ionic species simultaneously, setting total current = 0 (the physiological definition of the resting/steady-state membrane potential when multiple ion species contribute) gives the general Goldman equation:

\[ E_R = V_m(I=0) = \frac{RT}{F}\ln\left(\frac{\sum_i P_{C_i^+}[C_i^+]_{out} + \sum_j P_{C_j^-}[C_j^-]_{in}}{\sum_i P_{C_i^+}[C_i^+]_{in} + \sum_j P_{C_j^-}[C_j^-]_{out}}\right) \]

Specialized to the three physiologically dominant ions (Na⁺, K⁺, Cl⁻) — the GHK Voltage Equation:

\[ \boxed{E_{m} = \frac{RT}{F}\ln\left(\frac{P_{Na^+}[Na^+]_{out} + P_{K^+}[K^+]_{out} + P_{Cl^-}[Cl^-]_{in}}{P_{Na^+}[Na^+]_{in} + P_{K^+}[K^+]_{in} + P_{Cl^-}[Cl^-]_{out}}\right)} \]

Note the asymmetry: for the cation terms (Na⁺, K⁺), outside concentrations go in the numerator and inside in the denominator; for the anion term (Cl⁻), it is reversed (inside in the numerator, outside in the denominator) — because current direction for an anion is opposite to that for a cation for the same concentration-gradient direction.

Key physiological permeability ratios given in the deck (relative to \(P_K = 1\)): - Resting state: \(P_K : P_{Na} : P_{Cl} = 1 : 0.03 : 0.1\) (K⁺ dominates → \(V_m\) close to \(E_K\)) - Active state (during the action potential upstroke): \(P_K : P_{Na} : P_{Cl} = 1 : 15 : 0.1\) (Na⁺ permeability transiently increases ~500-fold → \(V_m\) swings toward \(E_{Na}\))

4.4.6 GHK Equation Application: Dependence of RMP on \([K^+]_o\) and \(\alpha = P_{Na}/P_K\) [Deck I, Slide 15 of GHK section]

For a mammalian cell (RMP = −56 mV in the example given, with K⁺: 4 mM out/150 mM in; Na⁺: 145 mM out/5 mM in; Ca²⁺: 1.5 mM out/100 nM in; Cl⁻: 123 mM out/4.2 mM in): - If \(\alpha = P_{Na}/P_K = 0\) (no Na⁺ permeability at all), the GHK equation reduces exactly to the Nernst equation for K⁺, and \(V_m\) vs. \(\log[K^+]_o\) is a perfectly straight line with slope 61.5 mV per decade (the “Nernst slope”). - As \(\alpha\) increases (0.01, 0.03, 0.1 shown as example curves), the \(V_m\) vs. \([K^+]_o\) relationship deviates from linearity, especially at low \([K^+]_o\) (where \(P_{Na}\) contribution becomes relatively more significant). - Conclusion stated explicitly in the deck: “In normal physiological conditions, the GHK equation is more applicable than the Nernst equation” — because real membranes are never permeable to only one ion.

4.4.7 Real Experimental Application — Permeability Coefficients [Deck II, Slides 15–16]

Example data (Myxicola axons; Amphiuma red blood cells, Stoner & Kregenow 1980; via Bowman, J. Theor. Biol., 1984): permeability coefficients (cm/s) for K⁺ and Na⁺ were measured via influx, efflux, and I–V leakage-current fitting methods, showing that: - At rest, \(P_K \gg P_{Na}\) (e.g., resting \(P_K \sim 3\times10^{-6}\) cm/s vs. resting \(P_{Na}\sim3\times10^{-8}\) cm/s — roughly 100-fold difference). - Upon stimulation, both permeabilities increase by orders of magnitude, and the K⁺:Na⁺ permeability ratio compresses substantially (both increase, but Na⁺ increases proportionally much more), consistent with the resting → active permeability ratio shift described in §4.4.5. - The GHK equation, fit to real experimental I–V data (e.g., in Amphiuma red blood cells), can be used to back-calculate \(P_{Na}\), \(P_{Cl}\), \(P_K\) from measured current-voltage curves.

  10. GHK Voltage Equation: \(E_m = \dfrac{RT}{F}\ln\left(\dfrac{P_{Na}[Na^+]_{out}+P_K[K^+]_{out}+P_{Cl}[Cl^-]_{in}}{P_{Na}[Na^+]_{in}+P_K[K^+]_{in}+P_{Cl}[Cl^-]_{out}}\right)\)
  11. Permeability ratios: Resting \(P_K:P_{Na}:P_{Cl}=1:0.03:0.1\); Active \(=1:15:0.1\)

# Chapter 5 — Concept of Resting Membrane Potential (RMP), Maintenance of RMP: Pumps & Transporters; Exemplar Pump: Na⁺–K⁺ ATPase

5.1 Concept of Resting Membrane Potential (RMP)

5.1.1 Definition

The RMP is the steady-state transmembrane voltage of an unstimulated (non-firing) excitable cell — typically −60 to −70 mV (inside negative relative to outside) for a neuron. Physiologically, it is best understood as the potential at which the net transmembrane current from all permeant ion species sums to zero (Chapter 4, GHK voltage equation), not the equilibrium potential of any single ion in isolation.

5.1.2 Two Contributors to RMP — Passive vs. Active [Deck I, Slides 36–37]

Active Transport Passive Transport
Direction Against (up) concentration gradient Down (with) concentration gradient
Energy requirement Requires an energy source (ATP hydrolysis, or coupling to another ion’s downhill gradient) No direct energy source required
Examples Na⁺–K⁺ ATPase Selective ion channel permeability; passive ion distribution described by the Donnan equilibrium (Chapter 4, §4.3)

Key conceptual point: the Donnan equilibrium (§4.3) describes what the passive distribution of permeant ions would be, given fixed impermeant intracellular anions — but real cells are not at true Donnan/thermodynamic equilibrium; the Na⁺–K⁺ ATPase actively maintains a steady state that deviates from the passive Donnan prediction (especially for Na⁺), which is precisely why active pumping is necessary to prevent osmotic cell swelling/lysis and to maintain the physiological ion gradients that make excitability possible.

5.2 Role of Ionic Gradients — Why Gradient Maintenance Matters [Deck I, Slide 36]

  • Cells across the whole tree of life have conserved a similar cytoplasmic ionic composition: low Ca²⁺, low Na⁺, high K⁺, near-neutral pH (Mulkidjanian et al., 2012) — suggesting this ionic “signature” is an ancient, deeply conserved feature dating to the earliest cells.
  • Energy is stored in ionic gradients across the plasma membrane (an electrochemical “battery”).
  • Steep Na⁺ and K⁺ gradients power secondary active transport of sugars, neurotransmitters, amino acids, metabolites, and other ions (H⁺, Ca²⁺, Cl⁻) via symporters/antiporters (Chapter 3, §3.6).
  • Ion gradients enable rapid signaling via opening of Na⁺ or K⁺ channels in response to extracellular ligands or voltage changes.
  • Specific physiological examples given in the deck:
    • Kidney distal tubule cells use the Na⁺ gradient to filter blood, reabsorb glucose/amino acids, regulate electrolytes, and maintain pH.
    • Sperm cells rely on regulated ion/membrane-potential control for motility and the acrosome reaction.
    • Astrocytes use the Na⁺ gradient to drive neurotransmitter reuptake (e.g., glutamate transporters, EAATs).

5.3 Active Transport: The Sodium-Potassium Pump [Deck I, Slide 43; Deck II, Slide 2]

Both decks introduce the Na⁺–K⁺ pump with the framing: “Neuronal Signaling & Sodium-Potassium Pump” — making explicit that the pump is not just a housekeeping protein but the direct enabler of neuronal signaling, because it establishes the Na⁺/K⁺ gradients that the action potential (Chapter 6) subsequently exploits.

5.3.1 Basic stoichiometry [Supplemented — standard fact, consistent with deck’s ATPase cycle figure]

Per ATP hydrolyzed: 3 Na⁺ ions pumped out, 2 K⁺ ions pumped in — an electrogenic pump (net outward movement of 1 positive charge per cycle), which itself contributes a small hyperpolarizing component to RMP in addition to its indirect effect via maintaining the ionic gradients that set \(E_K\) and \(E_{Na}\).

5.4 The Na⁺–K⁺ ATPase Enzymatic Cycle (the “Post-Albers” or E1/E2 Cycle) [Deck II, Slide 5]

The pump cycles through eight numbered steps (as diagrammed in the deck, based on Clausen et al., 2017):

  1. The E1 form of the pump, with ATP bound, is empty and exposed to the intracellular space.
  2. Three Na⁺ ions from inside the cell bind to sites on E1.
  3. ATP is hydrolyzed, phosphorylating the α-subunit (→ E1P with 3 Na⁺ occluded). This phosphorylation causes a conformational change that occludes the 3 Na⁺ ions from both the cytosolic and extracellular space.
  4. The conformation spontaneously changes to E2 (→ E2P), exposing the bound Na⁺ to the extracellular space, allowing Na⁺ to exit.
  5. The E2-P form of the pump is now empty and exposed to the extracellular space.
  6. Two K⁺ ions from outside the cell bind to sites on E2.
  7. Phosphate (Pi) leaves E2, which changes its conformation once again and occludes the 2 K⁺ ions.
  8. A new ATP molecule binds to E2, causing a conformational change that returns the pump to its E1 state; K⁺ is now exposed to the cytosolic space and leaves E1, completing the cycle.

Condensed cycle notation (Deck II, Slide 6):

\[ E1\text{-}ATP\ (3\,Na) \rightleftharpoons E1P\text{-}ADP\ [3\,Na] \rightleftharpoons E2P\ (3\,Na) \underset{H}{\rightleftharpoons} E2P\ (2\,K) \rightleftharpoons E2\text{-}Pi\ [2\,K] \rightleftharpoons E1\text{-}ATP\ (3\,Na, \text{cycle repeats}) \]

with 2K, Pi, H, ATP consumed to return to the starting E1-ATP/3Na state.

5.5 Structural Conformational Changes During the Cycle [Deck II, Slide 6]

Based on crystal structures (Sus scrofa/pig ATP1a1, PDB codes 4H1W, 4HQJ, 4HYT, 3KDP), Clausen et al. (2017) describe:

  • After binding of three sodium ions, TM1 (transmembrane helix 1) rearranges to a position that blocks the cytoplasmic entrance pathway.
  • The cytoplasmic domains tighten around the phosphorylation site nucleotide, which reacts with Asp369 (D369) — the conserved catalytic aspartate.
  • Following sodium occlusion, ADP is released, and an extracellular pathway opens, allowing the three sodium ions to exit.
  • In the externally opened conformation (structure 4HYT), three ion-binding residues are directly visible from the outside, while the intracellular domains are completely wrapped around the phosphorylated D369.
  • Binding of two extracellular potassium ions initiates closure of the extracellular gate and dephosphorylation of D369.
  • A narrow pathway from the cytoplasm to the sodium-specific binding site is proposed to be a C-terminal proton path, used for charge conservation during the cycle.

Key residues highlighted: D804 and E327 (part of the ion-binding/access pathway machinery visible in the inwardly-opened conformation).

5.6 Pharmacology of the Na⁺–K⁺ ATPase [Deck II, Slide 5]

5.9 Thermodynamic Basis of Allosteric Protein Function — the AChR Gating Example [Deck II, Slide 3]

Presented as a general framework (relevant to gating of ligand-gated channels like the nicotinic acetylcholine receptor, AChR, and by extension to any allosteric membrane protein including pumps):

  • Cyclic thermodynamic scheme: states C (closed, unliganded) ⇌ AC (closed, liganded) ⇌ AO (open, liganded) ⇌ O (open, unliganded), forming a thermodynamic cycle with binding (\(K_d\), \(J_d\)) and gating (\(E_0\), \(E_1\)) equilibrium constants along each edge.
  • Because free energy is a state function, the cycle must satisfy: \(\Delta G_{LA} + \Delta G_1 = \Delta G_0 + \Delta G_{HA}\)
  • Defining \(\Delta G_B\) (the coupling free energy between binding and gating):

\[ \Delta G_B = \Delta G_{HA} - \Delta G_{LA} = \Delta G_1 - \Delta G_0 \]

  • The gating free energy: \(\Delta G_n = -RT\ln(E_n)\) (R = gas constant, T = absolute temperature; \(E_n\) = a gating equilibrium constant)
  • \(\Delta G_{LA/HA} = -RT\ln(1/K_d)\) or \(-RT\ln(1/J_d)\) (LA = low affinity/closed-state binding, HA = high affinity/open-state binding)

\[ \boxed{\Delta G_B = \Delta G_{HA} - \Delta G_{LA} = -0.59\ln(K_d/J_d)} \]

(at ~room temperature, \(RT \approx 0.59\) kcal/mol)

  • For two binding sites (e.g., the two non-α subunit interfaces α–δ and α–γ in muscle-type AChR): \(\Delta G_2 = \Delta G_0 + \Delta G_B^{\gamma/\varepsilon} + \Delta G_B^{\delta}\)
  • Key assumption stated in the deck: mutations away from the binding site have negligible effect on \(K_d/J_d\), and their (gating) effects are independent of each other (an additivity/separation-of-energy assumption central to mutant cycle analysis, Jadey & Auerbach, 2010).
  • The accompanying energy-landscape figure shows representative values: activation barrier ΔG‡, overall gating free energy ΔG₀ = −12.3 kcal/mol, per-site binding coupling energy 2ΔG_B, and a mutant-shifted landscape (ΔG₂ = −2.4 kcal/mol) — illustrating how ligand binding and mutations reshape the energy landscape governing channel/pump conformational transitions.

# Chapter 6 — Ion Channels; Hodgkin-Huxley Model for Na⁺ and K⁺ Channels; Action Potentials; Simulation by HHSim

6.1 Active Properties of Excitable Cells — Anatomy of the Action Potential [Deck II, Slide 17]

Standard terminology (defined against a resting membrane potential, \(V_{REST} \approx -68\) mV in the deck’s example):

  • Depolarization — membrane potential becomes less negative (moves toward 0 mV and beyond).
  • V_threshold — the critical depolarization level at which the regenerative, all-or-none spike is triggered.
  • Overshoot — the portion of the spike where \(V_m\) becomes positive (crosses 0 mV).
  • Repolarization — the falling phase, membrane potential returns toward rest.
  • Spike height — often measured at 50% level, from threshold/baseline to peak.
  • Hyperpolarization / AHP (after-hyperpolarization) — the membrane potential transiently dips below RMP after the spike, before returning to RMP (\(E_K\) marks the theoretical lower bound the AHP approaches, since it is driven mainly by persistent K⁺ conductance).
  • The vertical axis in the deck’s schematic spans from \(E_K\) (bottom) to \(E_{Na}\) (top), with RMP (−68 mV) and 0 mV marked — emphasizing that the action potential is fundamentally the membrane voltage swinging between the Nernst potentials of the two dominant ions, Na⁺ and K⁺.

6.2 “What Do We Know Today?” — The Modern Mechanistic Picture [Deck II, Slide 18]

The deck decomposes a single action potential waveform into sequential channel-gating events (matched against the underlying \(I_{Na}\) and \(I_K\) current traces):

  1. Na⁺ channels open (initial fast depolarizing phase)
  2. More Na⁺ channels open (regenerative, accelerating upstroke — positive feedback)
  3. Na⁺ channels close (inactivation sets in near the peak)
  4. K⁺ channels open (delayed relative to Na⁺; drives repolarization)
  5. K⁺ channels close (as \(V_m\) returns toward rest, ending the after-hyperpolarization phase)

This is the conceptual skeleton for the full Hodgkin-Huxley (HH) model, which formalizes each of these steps quantitatively using gating variables (\(m\), \(h\), \(n\)).

6.3 Ionic Currents & the Equivalent Circuit Model of the Membrane [Deck I, Slide 15 & Deck II, Slide 20–22]

6.3.1 Basic RC circuit

The membrane is modeled as a resistor (R_m) and capacitor (C_m) in parallel, in series with a battery (\(E_m\), the driving potential):

  • \(R_m\) = membrane resistance
  • \(E_m\) = potential difference (driving force)
  • \(C_m\) = membrane capacitance

6.3.2 Capacitive current

Charge relates to potential via \(q = C_mV_m\), so:

\[ I_c = \frac{dq}{dt} = C_m\frac{dV_m}{dt} \]

This arises because the lipid bilayer is a thin, non-conductive dielectric separating two conductive solutions (extracellular fluid and cytoplasm) — a textbook parallel-plate capacitor geometry.

6.3.3 Ionic currents — Kirchhoff’s Current Law for the full circuit

\[ I_{ion} = I_{Na^+} + I_{K^+} + I_{Ca^{2+}} + I_{Cl^-} \]

Total membrane current (Kirchhoff’s law applied to the equivalent circuit, each ionic branch modeled as a conductance \(g_X\) in series with a battery \(E_X\)):

\[ I_m = C_m\frac{dV_m}{dt} + I_{ion}(V,t) \]

6.4 Membrane Rectification Properties [Deck II, Slide 23]

Real voltage-gated channels show clear deviation from Ohmic (\(I \propto V\)) behavior on an I–V plot: - Outward rectifier — conducts more easily in the outward direction (steeper I–V slope for \(V>0\)) - Inward rectifier — conducts more easily in the inward direction - Ohmic conductor — straight-line I–V (reference case, not typical of real channels)

Example data shown: voltage-gated K⁺ channel response to depolarizing voltage steps (with and without dopamine, DA, modulation) shows sustained, slowly-activating outward current; voltage-gated Na⁺ channel shows a rapidly activating-and-inactivating transient inward current (~1 nA scale, ms timescale) with a characteristic bell-shaped peak-current-vs.-voltage curve.

6.5 The Hodgkin-Huxley (HH) Model — Full Derivation

6.5.1 The Experimental System [Deck II, Slide 24]

Two technical breakthroughs enabled HH’s quantitative analysis: 1. The giant squid axon (diameter ~0.5 mm) — large enough to permit insertion of electrodes directly into the axoplasm using the crude electronics of the 1950s. (Its utility for studying nerve properties was recognized by J.Z. Young in 1936.) 2. The voltage clamp — a feedback control device capable of holding the membrane potential at a desired, experimenter-set value.

6.5.2 Space Clamp — Eliminating the Axial (Spatial) Dependence [Deck II, Slide 25]

  • A pair of electrodes runs the length of (or stimulates uniformly along) the axon, achieving complete axial symmetry — i.e., \(V_m\) is made uniform (constant) at every point \(x\) along the axon at a given instant.
  • Result: this eliminates the axial/spatial term from the general cable equation, reducing it to a purely temporal (space-clamped) equation:

\[ C_M\frac{dV(x,t)}{dt} + I_{ion}(V(x,t),t) = i_{ext}(x,t) \]

which, under perfect space clamp, becomes independent of \(x\).

6.5.3 Voltage Clamp — Eliminating the Capacitive Current [Deck II, Slides 26–29]

  • A feedback amplifier compares the measured membrane voltage \(V_m\) to a command voltage \(V_c\) set by the experimenter, and injects whatever current is needed to force \(V_m = V_c\) (i.e., to “clamp” the voltage).
  • Because \(V_m\) is held constant once the step is applied, \(dV_m/dt = 0\) during the plateau of the step, so \(I_c = C_m\,dV_m/dt \to 0\) — the capacitive current is confined to a brief transient immediately after the voltage step, after which the injected current equals (the negative of) the ionic current, which can therefore be measured directly and cleanly.
  • Typical experimental trace (Deck II, Slide 27): an 85 mV depolarizing step (from −65 mV rest to +20 mV clamp voltage) evokes: (i) a brief capacitive current spike at \(t=0\); (ii) a transient inward current (Na⁺, peaking around ~1 ms); (iii) a delayed, sustained outward current (K⁺, the “delayed outward current,” growing over several ms and staying elevated).
  • Isolating the Na⁺ and K⁺ components (Deck II, Slide 28): by using ion-substitution protocols (e.g., replacing external Na⁺ to abolish the Na⁺ current at a given clamp voltage, leaving only \(I_K\)), Hodgkin and Huxley could measure \(I_K\) alone, then obtain \(I_{Na} = I_m - I_K\) by subtraction from the total current trace. Capacitive + leak currents (\(I_C+I_L\)) form a fast initial transient on a much shorter timescale (µs) than the ionic currents (ms).
  • Series of voltage-clamp steps (Deck II, Slide 29): stepping to a range of clamp voltages (26, 39, 52, 65, 78 mV depolarizing steps in the example) produces a family of current traces, each showing the initial brief capacitive spike followed by growing sustained outward current — the larger the depolarization, the larger and faster-developing the outward K⁺ current.

6.5.4 Selective Pharmacological Block — Identifying Current Carriers [Deck II, Slide 30]

Toxin Selectively blocks
TTX (Tetrodotoxin, from pufferfish) Sodium channels
TEA (Tetraethylammonium) Potassium channels

Applying TTX to a control trace eliminates the fast transient inward component, leaving only the slower sustained outward K⁺ current; applying TEA eliminates the sustained outward component, leaving only the fast transient inward Na⁺ current — this pharmacological dissection is definitive evidence that two pharmacologically and kinetically distinct channel types carry the two current components.

6.5.5 Na⁺ Channel Response and I–V Relationship [Deck II, Slide 31]

A family of step-voltage responses shows the Na⁺ current is transient (activates then inactivates within a few ms) at every voltage tested; plotting peak Na⁺ current vs. voltage produces a characteristic curve that is inward (negative) at moderate depolarizations, reaches a peak (most negative) around −20 to −30 mV, and then reverses sign near \(E_{Na}\) (+55 to +60 mV) — the classic N-shaped Na⁺ I–V curve.

6.5.6 HH Formalism — The Parallel Conductance Model [Deck II, Slides 32, 39–40]

HH proposed the membrane current is the sum of four independent parallel branches:

\[ I_m = C_m\frac{dV_m}{dt} + g_{Na}(V_m-V_{Na}) + g_K(V_m-V_K) + g_{Cl}(V_m-V_{Cl}) \]

i.e., \(I_m = I_{channel} + I_{capacitance}\), \(I_{channel} = I_{Na}+I_K+I_L\) (leak).

Each ionic branch obeys Ohm’s Law: \(I_X = G_X(V_m-E_X)\) for \(X\in\{Na,K,L\}\), where the reversal (Nernst) potential \(E_X = \dfrac{RT}{zF}\ln\!\left(\dfrac{X_{out}}{X_{in}}\right)\).

The crucial insight: unlike a fixed resistor, the conductance itself is time- and voltage-dependent:

\[ G_X = G_X^{Max}\cdot F(V(t),t) \]

Goal (explicitly stated in the deck): experimentally determine both \(G_X^{Max}\) (the maximum conductance) and the functional form \(F(V(t),t)\) (the gating variable(s)) for each channel type.

6.5.7 Measured Conductance Waveforms [Deck II, Slide 41]

  • \(G_K\) vs. time, at a family of depolarization levels (6 to 109 mV): rises with a sigmoidal (“S-shaped”) delay then saturates at a plateau — no inactivation (stays elevated as long as depolarization is maintained).
  • \(G_{Na}\) vs. time, same depolarization range: rises rapidly then decays (inactivates) even though the depolarizing voltage step is still held — a transient conductance, in contrast to \(G_K\)’s sustained/non-inactivating conductance.

6.5.8 The Gate Model — Formal Kinetic Scheme [Deck II, Slides 34–37]

Assumptions of the gate model: - The membrane contains aqueous pores through which ions flow passively down their concentration gradients. - These pores contain voltage-sensitive gates that open/close depending on transmembrane potential. - The closed ⇌ open transition follows first-order kinetics with voltage-dependent rate constants \(\alpha(V)\) (closed→open) and \(\beta(V)\) (open→closed):

\[ \text{closed} \underset{\alpha(V)}{\overset{\beta(V)}{\rightleftharpoons}} \text{open} \]

Let \(P\) = fraction of gates open at time \(t\); then \((1-P)\) = fraction closed:

\[ \frac{dP}{dt} = \alpha(V)(1-P) - \beta(V)P \]

Steady state (\(dP/dt=0\)):

\[ P_\infty(V) = \frac{\alpha(V)}{\alpha(V)+\beta(V)} \]

Time-dependent solution (single exponential approach to steady state):

\[ P(t) = P_\infty - (P_\infty - P_0)e^{-t/\tau(V)}, \qquad \tau(V) = \frac{1}{\alpha(V)+\beta(V)} \]

Rearranged for \(\alpha,\beta\) in terms of experimentally-fit \(P_\infty\) and \(\tau\):

\[ \alpha(V) = \frac{P_\infty(V)}{\tau(V)}, \qquad \beta(V) = \frac{1-P_\infty(V)}{\tau(V)} \]

Multiple gates: if a channel requires all of \(n\) identical independent gating particles to be open simultaneously for conduction, the probability of channel opening scales as \((P(t))^n\).

Gate classification: - Activation gate — \(P(t,V)\) increases with depolarization - Inactivation gate — \(P(t,V)\) decreases with depolarization

6.5.9 Fitting m, n, and h [Deck II, Slides 42–43]

HH found empirically that the time-dependent conductances fit as:

\[ G_{Na}(V,t) = \bar{g}_{Na}\, m^3(t,V)\, h(t,V), \qquad G_K(V,t) = \bar{g}_K\, n^4(t,V) \]

  • \(m\) = Na⁺ activation gating variable (3 identical gates, hence \(m^3\))
  • \(h\) = Na⁺ inactivation gating variable (1 gate)
  • \(n\) = K⁺ activation gating variable (4 identical gates, hence \(n^4\))

Each obeys the standard first-order gate kinetics:

\[ \frac{dm}{dt} = \alpha_m(1-m)-\beta_m m, \quad \frac{dn}{dt} = \alpha_n(1-n)-\beta_n n, \quad \frac{dh}{dt}=\alpha_h(1-h)-\beta_h h \]

with exponential-approach solutions \(m(t) = m_0 - (m_0-m_\infty)(1-e^{-t/\tau_m})\) (and similarly for \(n\), \(h\)).

Activation vs. inactivation defined via the direction of the exponential relaxation: - Activation: \(x_\infty > x_0\) (the gating variable increases toward its new steady state upon depolarization — true for \(m\) and \(n\)) - Inactivation: \(x_\infty < x_0\) (the gating variable decreases upon sustained depolarization — true for \(h\))

Time courses (from the figure, Deck II Slide 43): upon a depolarizing voltage step, \(m\) rises very rapidly (fast activation), \(h\) falls more slowly (slower inactivation), and \(n\) rises slowly (delayed activation) — this relative timing (\(m\) fast, \(h\) and \(n\) slower) is exactly what produces the characteristic action potential shape: a fast Na⁺-driven upstroke followed by Na⁺ inactivation and delayed K⁺-driven repolarization.

6.5.10 Experimental Measurement of Steady-State (In)activation & Recovery [Deck II, Slide 44]

Two-pulse voltage protocols are the standard experimental method: - Steady-state inactivation (availability) curve: a long conditioning “prepulse” to various voltages, followed by a fixed test pulse; plotting \(I/I_{max}\) vs. prepulse voltage produces the classic sigmoidal (Boltzmann) h∞ curve. (The deck example compares wild-type, WT, vs. a Naᵥ channel mutant, R1629Q, showing the mutant’s inactivation curve is left-shifted, i.e., inactivates at more hyperpolarized voltages — a gain-of-function-adjacent biophysical phenotype relevant to channelopathies.) - Recovery from inactivation: a paired-pulse protocol (P1, then P2 after a variable interpulse interval) measures \(P2/P1\) current ratio vs. interpulse interval — reveals the time course of recovery from inactivation (again, WT vs. R1629Q mutant compared, mutant showing slower/right-shifted recovery kinetics).

6.5.11 Determining \(\alpha_x\) and \(\beta_x\) — the Empirical Curve-Fitting Step [Deck II, Slide 45]

Procedure: (1) determine \(x_\infty(V)\) and \(\tau_x(V)\) experimentally at each clamp voltage (illustrated for K⁺ conductance \(g_K\) at \(V_c=22\) mV and \(V_c=-2\) mV); (2) back-calculate \(\alpha_x\) and \(\beta_x\) using \(\alpha_x = x_\infty/\tau_x\) and \(\beta_x = (1-x_\infty)/\tau_x\); (3) fit smooth empirical functions of \(V\) to the resulting \(\alpha_x(V)\), \(\beta_x(V)\) data points across many voltages.

HH’s original empirical fitted functions (squid giant axon, standard HH formulation, \(V\) in mV, rates in ms⁻¹; note sign/offset conventions can vary between textbook presentations — these are exactly as given in the deck):

\[ \alpha_m(V) = \frac{0.1(-V+25)}{e^{(-V+25)/10}-1}, \qquad \beta_m(V) = 4e^{-V/18} \]

# Chapter 7 — Ion Channel Structure, Function & Modulation; Methods to Study Ion Channels and Receptors (Patch-Clamp, Planar-Bilayer, Crystallography, Cryo-EM)

7.1 Biophysical Properties of Ion Channels — Overview Framework [Deck II, Slide 53]

The deck organizes everything you need to know about a given ion channel into six core biophysical properties, radiating from the channel itself:

  1. Permeation — how ions physically move through the open pore (rate, selectivity filter chemistry, single-channel conductance).
  2. Selectivity — the channel’s preference for one ion species over others (e.g., K⁺ over Na⁺ in K⁺ channels, despite Na⁺ being physically smaller — the classic “selectivity paradox” resolved by the geometry/chemistry of the selectivity filter, first shown structurally for KcsA by MacKinnon’s group).
  3. Gating — the conformational mechanism(s) that open/close the channel (voltage, ligand, mechanical force, temperature, phosphorylation — see Chapter 3 §3.7, TREK1 example).
  4. Cooperativity — interactions between subunits or between multiple binding sites that make responses non-linear/switch-like (e.g., Hill-coefficient behavior in multimeric channels).
  5. Post-translational modification — phosphorylation, glycosylation, palmitoylation, ubiquitination, etc., which tune channel trafficking, gating, and stability.
  6. Plasticity — activity-dependent, long-term changes in channel number/properties (e.g., AMPA receptor trafficking underlying LTP/LTD).
  7. Inactivation / Desensitization — a distinct, typically voltage- or time-dependent loss of conductance despite continued stimulus (inactivation for voltage-gated channels; desensitization for ligand-gated channels, e.g., prolonged agonist exposure reducing nAChR responsiveness).

7.2 Structure–Function Themes Recap (Cross-referenced from Chapters 2–3)

  • Domain architecture determines function (Section 2.5): TM-helix count, pore-loop architecture, and cytosolic regulatory domains (NBDs, ATD, CTD) all correlate with gating mechanism and selectivity.
  • Superfamily phylogeny reflects shared ancestry and mechanism (Section 3.4): VGIC superfamily (Kᵥ, Naᵥ, Caᵥ, CNG, HCN, TRP) likely descends from a bacterial KcsA-like leak K⁺ channel ancestor; LGIC (pentameric Cys-loop-type) superfamily (nAChR, GABA_A, GlyR, 5-HT₃) shares a common pentameric fold, including bacterial homologs (GLIC, ELIC).
  • Polymodal gating (Section 3.7, TREK1) shows a single channel protein can integrate voltage, mechanical, thermal, chemical (lipid, pH), and enzymatic (phosphorylation) inputs simultaneously.

7.3 Patch-Clamp Electrophysiology

7.3.1 Instrumentation Overview [Deck II, Slide 19 & 51]

A complete patch-clamp rig (as diagrammed in both slides) comprises: - Inverted phase-contrast microscope — to visualize the cell and position the pipette - Vibration isolator / vibration-free table — mechanical isolation needed because the pipette-to-cell seal is extremely sensitive to vibration (gigaohm-scale seals, see §7.3.2) - Faraday cage — electrically shields the sensitive high-impedance recording setup from ambient electrical noise - Amplifier (with PROBE headstage, current I and voltage V/STIM IN inputs) — the core patch-clamp amplifier - AD/DA converter — converts between analog signals and digital data for computer acquisition - Oscilloscope — real-time visual monitoring of current/voltage traces - Picoinjector — for precise pressure application (e.g., forming/breaking the seal, applying drugs locally) - Temperature controller — maintains stable, physiological (or controlled) bath temperature - Data acquisition work station — computer for recording, storing, and analyzing data

7.3.2 Patch-Clamp Configurations [Deck II, Slide 52]

The classical gigaseal patch-clamp method (Neher & Sakmann) proceeds through a defined sequence of steps:

Step I — Seal formation: A clean glass micropipette is pressed against the cell membrane; gentle suction forms a gigaseal (seal resistance 10–100 GΩ, i.e., gigaohm; note the deck’s earlier reference figure lists an initial “seal resistance 100 MΩ” stage before the gigaseal is achieved) between the pipette tip and the membrane patch — this high resistance is what allows detection of the tiny currents (picoamps) flowing through single ion channels, by minimizing leak current around the seal.

Step II — Cell-attached patch: The pipette remains sealed to the intact cell; recordings reflect only the small membrane patch under the pipette tip, while the rest of the cell remains physiologically intact (intracellular signaling machinery undisturbed).

From the cell-attached configuration, three further configurations can be obtained:

  • Step III — Whole-cell patch: Additional suction ruptures the patch of membrane under the pipette tip, creating continuity between the pipette interior and the cell cytoplasm. This allows recording of the summed current from the entire cell membrane and allows the pipette solution to dialyze/exchange with the cytoplasm (useful for controlling intracellular ionic composition, but also means soluble intracellular factors can wash out over time).
  • Step IV — Outside-out patch: After forming a cell-attached patch, the pipette is pulled away from the cell; the membrane patch reseals with its extracellular face now oriented outward (facing the bath) — useful for studying ligand-gated channels’ responses to different extracellular agonist concentrations, since the bath solution can be rapidly changed.
  • Step V — Inside-out patch: From the cell-attached configuration, the pipette is pulled away without first rupturing the patch fully into whole-cell mode (or via a different excision route as diagrammed); the excised patch reseals with its cytoplasmic face exposed to the bath — useful for studying how intracellular second messengers, ATP, Ca²⁺, or phosphorylation directly affect channel gating, since the bath (formerly “outside,” now facing the cytoplasmic side of the patch) can be freely manipulated.

7.3.4 Example Recordings Enabled by Patch-Clamp [Deck II, Slide 19]

The deck shows patch-clamp current traces for two very different transport proteins, illustrating that the technique is not limited to “classical” channels: - ATP7B (a Cu²⁺-ATPase, a P-type ATPase transporter, mutated in Wilson’s disease) — shows a transient current upon 100 µM ATP application, with amplitude/kinetics sensitive to pH (pH 6 vs. pH 7.8 traces compared, tens-of-pA scale). - SERCA (the sarco/endoplasmic reticulum Ca²⁺-ATPase) — shows a larger transient current (nA scale) upon 100 µM ATP application, again pH-sensitive (pH 7 vs. pH 7.8).

7.4 Planar Lipid Bilayer Recording [Supplemented — named in syllabus but not detailed in decks]

  • A planar (black) lipid bilayer is formed across a small aperture (typically tens to hundreds of µm) in a partition separating two aqueous chambers, either by “painting” a lipid/solvent mixture across the aperture or by apposing two lipid monolayers (Montal-Mueller method).
  • Purified/reconstituted channel proteins (or channel-forming toxins) are incorporated into the bilayer, and current is recorded under voltage-clamp conditions exactly as in patch-clamp, but without a live cell — this is especially useful for:
    • Studying purified, reconstituted channel proteins in a fully defined lipid environment (control over exact lipid composition).
    • Studying channels normally found in intracellular membranes (e.g., RyR, IP₃R) that are otherwise inaccessible to a patch pipette on the plasma membrane.
    • Testing effects of specific lipids (e.g., cholesterol, PIP₂) on channel gating in isolation from other cellular factors.
  • Limitation relative to patch-clamp: larger background noise/leak (lower achievable seal resistance than a gigaseal), and loss of the normal cytoskeletal/signaling context of the channel.

7.5 X-ray Crystallography of Ion Channels [Supplemented, tied to Deck I Slide 20’s KcsA reference]

  • Ion channel structural biology was transformed by Roderick MacKinnon’s 1998 crystal structure of the KcsA bacterial K⁺ channel (Nobel Prize in Chemistry, 2003), which for the first time showed atomic detail of the selectivity filter — a narrow region lined by backbone carbonyl oxygens that mimic the hydration shell of K⁺, allowing dehydrated K⁺ ions to pass while excluding the smaller Na⁺ (whose hydration shell is not as well mimicked by the filter geometry).
  • Subsequent landmark structures include voltage-gated K⁺ channels (KvAP, Shaker-family homologs), showing the voltage-sensor domain (S1–S4 helices, with S4 carrying gating-charge arginines) coupled to the pore domain (S5–S6, with the P-loop selectivity filter) — this connects directly to the HH gating-variable formalism (Chapter 6): the physical voltage sensor is the molecular correlate of the abstract \(m\), \(n\), \(h\) gating variables.
  • The Na⁺-K⁺ ATPase structures cited in Chapter 5 (PDB 4H1W, 4HQJ, 4HYT, 3KDP) are themselves products of X-ray crystallography, illustrating how this method resolved the pump’s E1/E2 conformational cycle at atomic resolution.
  • Limitations of crystallography for membrane proteins: requires the protein to be solubilized (typically in detergent) and coaxed into a well-ordered 3D crystal lattice — historically difficult for large, flexible, or conformationally heterogeneous membrane proteins, and the crystal environment/detergent may not perfectly reflect the native lipid bilayer or capture all physiologically relevant conformational states (often only captures one “snapshot” state per crystal).

7.6 Cryo-Electron Microscopy (Cryo-EM) of Ion Channels [Supplemented]

  • Cryo-EM (especially single-particle cryo-EM) has, since roughly 2013–2017 (the “resolution revolution,” enabled by direct electron detectors and improved image-processing algorithms), become the dominant method for solving structures of large, flexible membrane protein complexes — including many ion channels and transporters — without the need to grow crystals.
  • Method in brief: purified protein (often reconstituted in nanodiscs or detergent micelles to mimic a lipid environment) is rapidly frozen in a thin layer of vitreous (non-crystalline) ice; thousands to millions of individual particle images are collected by electron microscopy and computationally averaged/classified to reconstruct a 3D density map, into which an atomic model is built.
  • Key advantages for ion channels:
    • Can resolve multiple distinct conformational states (e.g., closed, open, inactivated) from a single, heterogeneous sample, via computational 3D classification — directly relevant to capturing the E1/E2 or activation/inactivation states discussed in Chapters 5–6.
    • Does not require crystallization, so is far more tractable for large, flexible, or difficult-to-crystallize channels (e.g., TRP channels, many of which were first solved by cryo-EM rather than crystallography).
    • Can be combined with native lipids/nanodiscs to better preserve physiologically relevant lipid-channel interactions.
  • Together, crystallography + cryo-EM + patch-clamp/planar-bilayer electrophysiology form a complementary toolkit: structural methods (crystallography, cryo-EM) show where the atoms are in defined (often static) conformational states; electrophysiological methods (patch-clamp, planar bilayer) show how the channel behaves functionally in real time (gating kinetics, conductance, selectivity, pharmacology) — the modern field integrates both to build a complete structure-function picture.

7.7 Modulation of Ion Channel Function — Consolidated View

Tying together material from across the decks (Chapter 3 §3.7 TREK1; Chapter 5 pharmacology; Chapter 6 gating), ion channel modulation operates through:

  1. Direct ligand binding (orthosteric — e.g., ACh on nAChR; or allosteric — e.g., benzodiazepines on GABA_A)
  2. Voltage sensing (intrinsic voltage-sensor domains, HH-type gating)
  3. Second messengers (cAMP/cGMP directly gating CNG/HCN channels; or indirectly via kinases, e.g., PKA/PKC phosphorylation of TREK1)
  4. Mechanical force (membrane stretch/tension — PIEZO, TRAAK, TREK1)
  5. Temperature (TRP channels; e.g., TRPV1 = heat/capsaicin receptor)
  6. pH (extracellular and intracellular, e.g., ASICs, TREK1)
  7. Toxins/pharmacological agents (TTX, TEA, ouabain, palytoxin — Chapters 5–6)
  8. Post-translational modification & trafficking (phosphorylation, palmitoylation, glycosylation; regulated insertion/removal from the membrane)
